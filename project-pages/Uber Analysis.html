Abstract¶

Car culture is a constant and immovable part of life while living in the United States of America. While efforts are continuously made to promote public transportation, the freeways are only getting bigger with more and more lanes that do not help with congestion at all. Bus stops and train stations are scarcely used for local transportation and people rely on more modern taxi services, namingly, Uber. The personalization of your pickup location is easier than ever with rideshare apps and Uber leads the charge.

This project analyzes a 2024 Uber ride-booking dataset to explore what factors (including ride distance, time of day, day of week, and vehicle type) influence booking value. Exploratory analysis and multiple supervised learning models (logistic regression, QDA, KNN, and linear regression) were applied to test these relationships. Contrary to expectations based on real-world ride pricing, no meaningful relationships were found between booking value and the trip characteristics examined; all classification models performed at or near chance level (AUC ≈ 0.50). However, clear patterns emerged in trip volume, with distinct peak hours around 10 AM and 6 PM, suggesting demand-based patterns even where price does not vary accordingly.

1. Introduction¶

With this dataset, I became curious about the different conclusions I could make, such as what factors are relevant for high customer ratings, or what are the biggest factors for driver cancellation, what elements of the pickup and drop off location or time increase the price of a ride. As someone who tends to be wary of ridesharing alone and also obtaining a ride for as cheap as possible, optimizing the factors that will supply me with the most satisfying ride in ride completion, booking value, and driver ratings.

I aim to discover what ride times generate the most revenue, and analyze the average fare and tip amount per passenger to see how group size impacts revenue. Past Uber and rideshare studies generally find that distance and time-of-day surge pricing are strong drivers of fare, so I aimed to test whether similar patterns existed in this dataset. My exploratory analysis focused on trip volume by hour and day of week, and on identifying which features correlate with booking value. I then applied several supervised learning methods (linear regression, logistic regression, QDA, and KNN) to test whether price range or vehicle type could be predicted from trip features. The key finding was that, despite clear patterns in when people request rides, booking value itself appears statistically independent of distance, travel time, vehicle type, and time of day in this dataset.

2. Data¶

This data set includes detailed ride-sharing data from Uber operations for the year 2024, capturing 148,770 total bookings across multiple vehicle types. I came across this dataset on Kaggle from the Kaggle user Yash dev Laddha (https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/yashdevladdha/uber-ride-analytics-dashboard). This dataset intrigued me with its most recent update being from 10 months ago, inspiring confidence in me for its currency. It is described that this dataset was collected from Uber operation's public ride-sharing data for the year of 2024.

Studying the data set lends itself to assisting Uber in making operation decisions that will minimize cancellations, improve wait times, and generally optimize a user's and driver's experience of riding with Uber. When inefficiencies in operation appear, it is potential for lost revenue for Uber, lost income for the driver, and a customer who is stranded and unable to go where they need to go. By eliminating inefficiencies, Uber can reduce cancellations of bookings, retain drivers, and create loyal customers that have the most satisfactory experience with Uber.

The author of the data notes that the key statistics highlighted in the data set are:

  • Total Bookings: 148.77K rides

  • Success Rate: 65.96% (93K completed rides)

  • Cancellation Rate: 25% (37.43K cancelled bookings)

  • Customer Cancellations: 19.15% (27K rides)

  • Driver Cancellations: 7.45% (10.5K rides)

And these are only 5 out of 21 columns mentioned in the data set. This project primarily used Date, Time, Booking Status, Vehicle Type, Booking Value, Ride Distance, and Avg CTAT.

3. Methodology¶

This project utilizes supervised learning.

This project uses supervised learning to test for predictive relationships between trip characteristics and two outcomes: booking value and vehicle type. After filtering to completed rides and engineering features (combined datetime, pickup hour, day of week), I applied linear regression to test whether ride distance predicts booking value, logistic regression, quadratic discriminant analysis (QDA), and K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) to test whether vehicle type could be classified from ride distance and booking value, and separately whether price class (low/mid/high) could be classified from ride distance, average CTAT, pickup hour, and vehicle type

All classification models were evaluated using accuracy and multi-class ROC AUC. Features were standardized prior to fitting KNN and QDA. An 80/20 train-test split was used throughout, stratified by the target class to preserve class proportions.

        roc_auc2 = auc_func(fpr, tpr)
        ax.plot(fpr, tpr, label=f"{cls} (AUC={roc_auc2:.3f})")
    ax.plot([0,1],[0,1],'k--', label='Random')
    ax.set_title(name)
    ax.set_xlabel('False Positive Rate')
    ax.set_ylabel('True Positive Rate')
    ax.legend(fontsize=8)

plt.suptitle('Multi-class ROC: Predicting Fare Class (Low/Mid/High)')
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

As you can see, the Logistic Regression, QDA, and KNN models all produce ROC curves representing the range of price for booking an Uber that lie almost entirely on top of the diagonal line, the "Random" line. There are AUC values clustered tightly around 0.50. This means that none of the models can confidently distinguish between a low price, mid price, or high price Uber ride better than a random guess while using the features of Ride Distance, Avg CTAT, hour of the day, or vehicle type to aid in its prediction ability. There is a slight variation in QDA's curve than the other models and their lines, but it does not represent anything meaningful.

To attempt to predict the price class (low, mid, or high) of an Uber ride upon booking using my current trip characteristics is not so successful regardless of the different model types.

Conclusion and Future Study¶

This project set out to identify which trip characteristics (ride distance, time of day, day of week, vehicle type, and average travel time) most influence Uber booking value and trip outcomes. Across extensive exploratory analysis and multiple supervised learning approaches (linear regression, logistic regression, QDA, and KNN), no meaningful relationship was found between booking value and any of these trip characteristics. Correlations between distance and fare were near zero, average fares were nearly identical across vehicle types and time-of-day bins, and all classification models predicting price class (low/mid/high) performed at chance level (AUC ≈ 0.50). Attempts to predict vehicle type from distance and fare similarly failed to outperform a majority-class baseline.

While this outcome differs from what real-world rideshare pricing studies typically find, where distance, duration, and surge pricing are major fare drivers, the analysis did reveal a clear, consistent pattern in trip volume: ride requests peak around 10 AM and 6 PM, aligning with typical commute times, while volume by day of week showed comparatively little variation. This suggests that demand fluctuates predictably with time of day even though, in this dataset, price does not.

These findings suggest that Booking Value in this dataset may be synthetically generated independent of other trip fields, rather than reflecting a realistic pricing model tied to distance, demand, or vehicle type.

Future study could take several directions. First, applying this same methodology to a dataset with verified real-world pricing logic would serve as a useful comparison, to confirm that the modeling approach itself is sound and would detect a genuine distance-price relationship if one existed. If an updated or alternative version of this dataset becomes available with verified pricing logic, revisiting the regression and classification models on Booking Value would be worthwhile to see whether the same null results hold.
