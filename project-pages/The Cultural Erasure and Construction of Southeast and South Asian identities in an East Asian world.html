The Cultural Erasure and Construction of Southeast and South Asian identities in an East Asian world.

Amber Han

This study explores how marginalized Asian Americans from non-East Asian backgrounds navigate their ethnic identities within a U.S. consumer culture that equates “Asian” primarily with East Asian (Chinese, Japanese, Korean). Addressing a gap in sociological literature, the research asks: How marginalized Asian Americans describe their relationship with consumer goods targeted at Asians and Asian Americans? Three semi-structured interviews were conducted and analyzed using inductive coding. The findings reveal an emerging theory of the dual process of cultural absence and displacement. Participants experienced a scarcity of accessible cultural objects from their own ethnic heritages, which hindered early ethnic identity formation. Simultaneously, they were socialized into a mainstream “Asian American” consumer identity dominated by East Asian products like K-pop, anime, and Korean cosmetics. Engaging with this commercially powerful mainstream often required participants to sublimate their specific ethnic identities, leading to a fragmented sense of self and a default identification as “generally Asian American” or simply “American.” The study concludes that the monolithic “Asian American” label obscures significant intra-group diversity and perpetuates the cultural erasure of non-East Asian communities. These findings highlight the need for disaggregated data, more nuanced sociological research, and community resources that support the specific cultural and identity needs of marginalized Asian American groups

Introduction

This study seeks to explore how marginalized Asian Americans describe their relationship with consumer goods targeted at Asians and Asian Americans. This topic dawned on me as I, a Vietnamese woman, was eating a dinner consisting of instant ramen noodles, a Japanese dish, that came from a Korean brand, while I drank bottled Japanese tea beside it. I took a gaze around and found that I owned almost next to nothing that would signify my Vietnamese heritage, save for a few Vietnamese condiments tucked away in a cabinet. While I do not denounce any of the Asian items I own that are not Vietnamese, it did sadden me to see how little representation I had of my ethnic identity in my own home.

In response, I wanted to delve deeper into how Asian Americans, specifically South East Asian Americans and South Asian Americans, interact with Asian consumer goods and how they felt if their possessions reflected mine at all. This topic is important due to its platforming of South East Asian and South Asian American identities in sociology from a South East Asian lens myself. More often than not, sociology articles about Asian American identities that are adjacent to consumerism, cultural production via material goods, and consumer behavior are created from the lens of western non-Asian individuals and hold an outsider position that is clear in their articles.

Throughout this study, it will lead you through a literature review that selected the most relevant articles to this topic and will explain how they are crucial to understanding the data that will later be presented in the study. Second, there will be descriptions of the methods that were used to collect the data and why those methods were chosen. Next, the findings will divulge into the responses of 3 participants who answered a series of questions in efforts to find out if I could answer my main research question. Following will be a discussion of my findings in relation to the literature review, and last will be a conclusion that restates the importance of my findings and their significance.

Literature Review 

Across different literary works that I used to research this topic, I uncovered three main themes that reoccurred: Culture is greatly built by commercial products (Moloney and Hunt 2012; McDonnell 2023, Warde 2016), these cultural products are inherently racial and have social power (Banks 2024; McDonnell 2023; Shankar 2012), and that the Asian American identity is fragmented (Moloney and Hunt 2012; Warde 2015; Yao 2016). The last theme can be attributed to the fact that there is not enough sociological research, at least in my pursuit of articles for this research, that may contribute to building a more consolidated community.

The first theme is greatly delved into by McDonnell (2023) as she tackles cultural objects. Cultural objects are defined as “shared significance embodied in form,” meaningful expressions that are materially perceptible by others. This includes what we usually think of as material objects and people’s bodily expressions (McDonnell 2023). While there are large icons of culture that may separate one area from another, like the Empire State Building versus Big Ben versus the Eiffel Tower, versus Tokyo Tower, what fascinatingly differentiates cultures are the more subtle things. Objects influence social life indirectly through their taken-for-grantedness. “As ground, cultural objects have power by being incorporated into institutions and practice,constituting the built environment, and establishing situational context” (McDonnell 2023). Things like slang, fashion, style, music, greetings, and more are all used universally but are juxtaposed when compared from country to country. These are the elements that birth pride, nationalism, and culture.

And as the world rapidly progresses, more and more things are being generated and produced for people to interact with. To generalize societal culture at the present, we currently live in a world characterized by an abundance of commodities, and as a result, the significance of possessions intensifies and defines the current culture where possible (Warde 2015). For those that can afford to, to possess something of cultural value has become a cultural touchstone itself; You cannot participate in culture if you do not have the means to purchase or possess something that accesses culture, like owning a phone or speaking in a certain way.

To be a pillar of culture is to hold massive influence, and in capitalist societies, companies are always fighting to be their industry leader, win over the masses, and be the  pinnacle producer of culture. In this endeavor, people operate under and replicate social hierarchy in the ways they produce goods and services. Ethnographic studies of advertising have illustrated the localized ways in which this industry does not simply reflect existing values but actively formulates and creates them (Shankar 2012). The ways these hierarchies are manifested in consumer goods is intentional and only affords cultural power to specific groups of people. Over time, we have observed Black genres of music be co-opted and stolen to make the records sellable to White audiences or cowboys being associated as an Americana symbol when it originated from Mexican vaqueros. Purchasing power is also reflected in the way companies produce and market items, such as when foreign items are altered to sell better to certain populations. Banks (2024) mentions that “cultural producers and distributors supply products that match the tastes of groups that have the most money due to being large or having a significant proportion of middle-class members.” The reference to “middle-class members” veils the racial implications that come with that label, mainly those racial groups mainly consisting of White and Asian Americans in comparison to Black and Hispanic Americans, Native Hawaiians or Pacific Islanders, and American Indians or Alaska Natives (Kochhar 2024). Inherently, cultural objects are racial and access different levels of social power if one is in possession of a cultural object.

In relation to how cultural objects pertain to the Asian American community, there is little research available linking the two. What is found consistently, however, when Asian Americans are in discussion to topics adjacent to cultural objects, consumption, or production, there is a great fragmentation within the Asian American identity and who is or is not included in it. Found in a legal census and in a survey conducted by the Department of Treasury, there are inconsistent options for marking an Asian identity on both forms. On the federal survey, the specific ethnic groups are distinctly marked as separate identities while a legal census categorizes the various identities into a singular “Other” category (Yao 2016). This may be subliminal, but it encourages a misunderstanding of who is included in the Asian American category and what those marginalized identities are worth in comparison to other ethnic identities at a federal level.

I did come across an article that directly addressed my specific topic of Asian Americans and how they interact with consumer goods. Interestingly, it emphasized the ways in which Asian Americans themselves are unable to be consistent with which identities are associated with specific cultural objects. With the cultural object, the term ‘FOB,’ an acronym for “Fresh Off the Boat,” Moloney and Hunt (2012) find that Asian Americans cannot agree on who mainly are the owners of this title and what sort of image it is ascribed to. Some young Cambodian and Laotian respondents complain of ‘fobby Hong Kongers’. But at the same time, some Chinese American respondents described ‘thuggish’ young Cambodians who speak non-standard English as FOBs Moloney and Hunt (2012).

As aforementioned, there is a lack of sociological research on Asian Americans and their relationship to consumerism. Many of the articles I came across that were consumer, cultural object and cultural production adjacent, were from perspectives that were not from Asian Americans and posed their studies from a White American perspective looking into Asian American culture. Additionally, the articles often focused on subgroups of Asian American identities, specifically Chinese, Japanese, and Korean more than any other identity. In this research project, I explore how marginalized Asian Americans describe their relationship with consumer goods targeted at Asians and Asian Americans. As a Vietnamese American woman myself, I aspire for my research to contribute to expanding the catalog of sociological studies for marginalized Asian Americans and their consumer behavior.

Methods

        For this research question, I decided on conducting interviews with my participants and following the transcripts I would utilize line-by-line and inductive coding to analyze the data. The reason I landed on doing interviews over observations was due to the anecdotal nature of my questions and how my research question relies on personal feelings. The questions asked were structured to allow the participant to respond as much and as little as they’d like. The topics included day to day consumption habits, evolutions of thought over their lifetime, explorations of formative experiences in their childhood, personal opinions and more. These items would be unobtainable or difficult to discern if my collection of data solely relied on observing my participants. Line-by-line and inductive coding would allow the data to speak for itself rather than the information being extracted from it be clouded by personal biases and impulsive understandings I had of the data.

        My data collection consisted of 3 selected participants, comprised of personal friends of mine, consisting of my housemate Taneesha, who is in her fourth year at UCSB, and two alumni named Simon and Kaelin that I had known prior to enrollment at UCSB, such as Simon who I have known since elementary school and Kaelin through a mutual friend during high school. Taneesha is 21 years old and is a female Indian American, Simon is 21 years old and is a male Vietnamese American, and Kaelin is 22 years old and a female Filipina American. These three were immediately thought of when I decided to focus my research question on marginalized Asian American identities aside from Korean, Japanese, and Chinese identities. Additionally, I predicted they would be able to provide detailed answers based on my impressions of them and their involvement in extracurricular activities related to my research question. In previous conversations I have had casually and informally with Taneesha and Simon, they have been discussions of our relationships with race and ethnicity, and I was aware Kaelin had been involved in the Filipino student organization on campus in a leadership position as the Academic Resources Coordinator.

I obtained informed consent to assist in this research project by reaching out to them either face to face, or by personal messages. Once I began each interview, I repeated what my research question was to affirm they still wanted to participate and assured them that the responses they provided were not to be published in any official capacity to a general public and that pseudonyms will be used in place of their names.

        Due to my already built rapport with them, I did not have to go through the process of making the participants comfortable enough to open up to me to provide detailed anecdotes for responses. This provided both lengthy answers that answered the questions I asked that easily created interviews that persisted from 30 minutes to an hour. The content of their answers also allowed for easy transitions to subsequent questions listed on my question guide, even if the answers were not asked in the order listed on my guide. While speaking with them, I utilized technological tools such as voice memo applications on my phone that have a built-in transcription tool so I did not have to manually transcribe the interviews myself. Similarly, for the Zoom interviews, I recorded the meeting for later reference and activated the transcription feature to download the full transcriptions afterwards.

        I began the analysis of my data with cleaning up the transcripts, such as removing filler words, timestamps, and correcting areas where the transcription features misheard and misspelled a word or incorrectly marked the beginning and end of sentences. This gave me an opportunity to get familiar with the data and prime myself for the themes I would create while  line-by-line coding afterwards. The transcripts were placed in a table in the middle column and the rows were separated by the question asked. During my line-by-line coding, I read each line carefully and identified the portions that caught my attention and subsequently highlighted them. The leftmost column contained my impulsive interpretations and codes of the highlighted sentences. After completing this process for all three transcripts, I went over it an additional time to organize and categorize the codes I had made in the left hand column, and placed the more concise organization of them in the rightmost column. These clarified codes were also more intentionally highlighted different colors that were all related to specific themes that emerged from the transcripts and the initial codes. I kept these colors consistent as best as I could throughout all the transcripts to better see the similar codes that appeared and the distinct codes that belonged to each participant.

        Although I did my best to keep the focus of the interviews on the participants, my collective histories with each participant and my own ethnic identity itself likely influenced the answers they gave me and the length of each answer. For my research, I had preconceived notions on what the answers would be and the expectation that the findings would be similar to my personal feelings. Despite me being aware of this and trying to be as cautious as possible while doing my coding, there is still a chance that I was just searching for quotes and codes that fit my preconceived notions for the answer to this question. My identity as a Vietnamese American woman also applies to the demographics I targeted in my research question, so my participants engaged in talking about shared experiences that we likely would have as being both marginalized Asian Americans. Moreover, the participants used terminology specific to Asian American culture with no explanation on the assumption that I would understand the terms but may be unfamiliar to non-Asian American individuals.

Findings 

Culturally Unavailable

        One thing to examine would be the ways cultural and consumer goods of their own ethnicities were present in the lives of the participants and how it shaped their identities. The participants were not raised in particularly cultural family homes that were able to teach them fluency in their language, or were not introduced to wildly popular visual entertainment that taught what styles were trending. Instead, all three only had their families to rely upon to pass on and keep traditional culture in circulation, which for Simon, was a minimal amount of information to keep track of. Simon detailed most of his cultural identity formation being aligned with a general title of “American.” Simon has never felt particularly Vietnamese American over his American identity due to his limited exposure and lack of relationship to Vietnamese culture. He describes any genuine interaction with Vietnamese culture was minimal and his family, and when it was not from his family, it largely came in the form of racism and racist microaggressions with Vietnamese people being the subject. As a result, Simon describes that he feels “closer to [his] American side” than his Vietnamese side. Without any notable Vietnamese cultural objects or consumer goods to interact with, Simon did not have the opportunity to build a Vietnamese identity even passively if he was to not receive any help from his parents or grandparents beginning from such an early age.

        Comparably, Taneesha also reported a lack of Indian cultural and consumer goods that defined her life. The closest option available to her for Indian consumer goods were cosmetics and fashion from her mother and Indian food also cooked by her mother. Taneesha cites her mother as being a large reason for being as cultural as she possibly was as she grew up. At the same time, makeup and food only comprise a small portion of cultural items that people are able to purchase in the pursuit of becoming more cultured. With the only gate way to Indian culture being her own mother, it made accessing this culture more difficult if their relationship would be unstable at times and creates a subconscious association with uneasy feelings about her mother to Taneesha’s own participation in Indian culture. Taneesha explicitly says so in:

I usually get those products from my mom, which is she gets it from India…I don't make a lot of Indian food, but I love Indian food. I just associate it with my mom, and it's so difficult to make Indian food.

On the other hand, Kaelin quoted that her Filipino American identity was not as absent while she was still growing as a young girl, eating Filipino food from her family and greeting her elders with the mano-po (this is a traditional greeting that directly translates to “your hand, please” and is typically done for elders). Still, Kaelin reported that to develop a deeper connection to Filipino American culture and history, it was a task she needed to take initiative and cultivate a rich understanding and practice of culture. To do this, she immersed herself in the Filipino student organization Kapatirang Pilipino (KP) and became the Academic Resources Advisor for it and followed a passion for educating others on Filipino culture. While Kaelin self-reported her experience as a Filipina American, it was her dissatisfaction with the cultural consumer goods she was already exposed to at home that led her to be as invested in KP for multiple hours a week all the time.

Juxtaposing one’s Ethnicity versus “The Mainstream”

        Consistently over the course of the three interviews when I asked what identities they believe people associate with Asian consumer goods, the answers included Korea, Japan, and China more than any other ethnic identity. All three participants interacted minimally with their culture as they grew from childhood and only two began concerted efforts to cultivate an ethnic identity once attending college. On the other hand, the exposure that they all reported of consumer goods relating to either China, Japan, or Korea appeared frequently throughout their developmental years up until present day. In Taneesha’s case, non-Indian Asian consumer goods defined a lot of what she experienced growing up. She provided that:

For most of my life, it's usually been other Asian Americans who share the same interests as me. Like, watching anime or K-pop or consuming, Asian cuisine, that sort of thing and I think it made me have more of a complicated relationship with my Indian identity because I do identify as Indian…So for most of my life, I just identified as generally Asian American...Like, I knew I was Indian. I wouldn't hide it. I wasn't ashamed of it. But I just felt a little less Indian or a little othered… 

This small excerpt occurred in the very first question asked to Taneesha, and what stands out to me is the distinction Taneesha makes quite immediately with the terms “Asian American” and “Indian American.” The separation of the two is very normalised for Taneesha, despite India being the second most populated country in Asia and included in most definitions of Asian American on surveys about racial identity (2025). Taneesha implies here that being Indian is more different than just being Asian American, as in order to feel more Asian American, she had to feel “a little less Indian” in order to do so. With the presence of consumer items like K-pop or non-Indian Asian food, Taneesha’s identity formed with a strong positive relationship with them and stagnated any presence of a more prevalent Indian identity forming if one had the chance.

Watching the Goods Come and Go

        With the participants, I tap into their perspective on how people, both Asian and non-Asian, are consuming Asian consumer goods and what their opinions of that are. In Kaelin’s interview, we touched on the perception of Asian culture being closely tied to aesthetic trends surrounding material goods. On the topic of how often people consume Filipino goods, Kaelin offers,

“What I associate, like, most Asian American goods with, is, like, the food, and then our clothing. But people don't really wear Filipino clothing, like, at least it hasn't become trendy, you know, like how people always wear the Chinese dresses or Vietnamese dresses to dress Asian. Like, no one's wearing a Filipiniana. That is just unheard of. And maybe it hasn't hit the social medias, like trended, yet.”

Kaelin identifies a form of people representing culture as wearing cultural articles of clothing, however in the context of non-Asian people wearing culturally Asian clothing, it comes from a place of wanting to simply “dress Asian.” Oftentimes, Asian Americans do not dress in cultural clothing in order to be identified as Asian and do not necessarily dress in specifically Chinese or Vietnamese articles of clothing if they do not already belong to those cultures. Kaelin identifies these two cultures and their traditional dresses because they are easy cultural signifiers for non-Asian people to identify as generally Asian without needing to identify Filipino or other Asian cultures. She attributes this lack of acknowledgement of Filipino culture to trends occurring in social media. Social media is a very visual medium and to trend on social media is to get high visibility on a certain topic and thus an aesthetic is curated as a result, generating common images that people come to associate with the topic. As Kaelin states that Filipino culture has not trended, she implies that Chinese culture and Vietnamese culture has trended and become the aesthetic people associate with general Asian culture and Asian items of clothing.

Discussion

With the way my participants responded and uncovered their opinions, I found that they align in the way the literature review describes “cultural objects” (McDonnell 2013) and how my participants interacted with or did not interact with the cultural objects of the Asian American community. My participants were not always exposed to cultural objects from their ethnicity, but they succeeded in creating a personal identity and culture with the items surrounding them, like Taneesha thriving in friend circles that were also East Asian due to their shared interests of East Asian cultural objects.

In terms of content comparison to the articles in the literature review, my research question seems most similar to Molloney and Geoffrey (2012) article in the way they both examine how Asian American identities view each other and their consumption of cultural objects. I do not think my research does much to contrast the findings, but I think the differences in presentation are important to contribute and helps contextualize the headspaces of a lot of our similar subjects and participants. With my more anecdotal findings, I hope my research offers a more intimate perspective for people to understand and make connections with the data and the findings.

My findings are important because, as the literature review demonstrates, there is not enough sociological research focusing on the points of view from marginalized Asian Americans. Not only does the existence of my research merely fill a gap of knowledge, the contents of my findings encapsulate a more holistic and personal view of perspectives of marginalized Asian people.

My findings serve to bring to light the overlooked Southeast Asians and South

Asians and how much more research there is to be done on everything and everything for Southeast Asian Americans and South Asian Americans and propelling its existence into more

mainstream knowledge. As the journal AAPI Nexus: Policy, Practice and Community details,

“In comparison to other racial groups, empirical research on AAPIs is severely

lacking...The concern regarding data on SEAAs is not only the lack thereof but

also the necessity of more critical data, quantitative and qualitative, for policy

makers to use in effective decision-making and implementation” (Maramba 2011).

In addition to the disaggregation of marginalized Asian American identities from the Asian American title, there should also be more resources available that specifically uplift the marginalized Asian American communities, especially in schools that are homes to these marginalized communities.

According to 2022 data from the Asian Pacific American Caucus, the poverty rate was 13% for Native Hawaiians and Pacific Islanders, 6% for Filipino Americans, 25% for Burmese and 31% for Micronesian Americans not from Guam…Many Asian Americans are falling through the cracks in data representation and social services, making it difficult to evaluate accessibility of services for Asian Americans who need them” (Muhammad 2023).

The front leaders of Asian American identity do not encapsulate this reality and will continue turning a profit for itself to accumulate its cultural power. Resources like easier access to education in all grades from K-12 and throughout college are essential for lifting up communities that have been systemically excluded from the fully experiencing institutions that they can reap benefits from. These communities also are composed of several first to second generation college students, such as all three of my participants being first generation and having a minimal amount of experience navigating for college as their family was unable to assist them. Resources that acknowledge this struggle would be monumental in assisting them, such as touring marginalized Asian American students to college campuses to encourage them to pursue higher education.

The study had several limitations. While conducting it, the timeframe in which it was constructed simultaneously with Professor Victor Rios’s SOC 108F class made elements feel rushed and not as in depth as required of me. Additionally, I only have a pool of three participants that only scratch the surface of marginalized Asian Americans identities that are included in that term that would have strengthened my research. Identities like Laos, Cambodia, Pakistan, Thai, and several more would have been more than welcome in my study and would have provided a wider and more diverse cache of data to answer my research question better.

Conclusion 

        Through in-depth interviews with three participants, this study supports and extends the key themes identified in the literature: the centrality of cultural objects in building identity (McDonnell 2023; Warde 2016), the inherent racialization and social power of these objects (Banks 2024; Shankar 2012), and the resulting fragmentation of the Asian American identity (Moloney and Hunt 2012; Yao 2016).

The findings reveal an emerging theory of the dual process of cultural absence and displacement. When the participants experienced a significant lack of accessible cultural goods from their own ethnic heritages, this created rifts in forming an ethnic identity that could have happened during childhood. Moreover, while they were not engaging with cultural goods from their own ethnicity, the participants were consistently exposed to and socialized by a slew of commercial Asian goods that made it mainstream, composed of East Asian cultural goods (e.g., K-pop, anime, traditional dresses).

This study’s primary contribution is to center the lived experiences of marginalized Asian American communities, voices often absent or subsumed in broader sociological research on consumption. My research also calls for the disaggregation of marginalized Asian identities from the wider title to more accurately address issues these groups are having but are being ignored because of their lack of visibility.
